L15: ## General advice when using Reinforcement Learningcite8† L16: ### TL;DRcite9† L17: 
L18:   1. Read about RL and Stable-Baselines3 (SB3)
L19: 
L20:   2. Do quantitative experiments and hyperparameter tuning if needed
L21: 
L22:   3. Evaluate the performance using a separate test environment (remember to check wrappers!)
L23: 
L24:   4. For better performance, increase the training budget
L25: Like any other subject, if you want to work with RL, you should first read about it (we have a dedicated cite10†resource page to get you started) to understand what you are using. We also recommend that you read the Stable Baselines3 (SB3) documentation and do the cite11†tutorial†github.com . It covers basic usage and guides you towards more advanced concepts of the library (e.g. callbacks and wrappers).
L26: Reinforcement Learning differs from other machine learning methods in several ways. The data used to train the agent is collected through interactions with the environment by the agent itself (as opposed to, for example, supervised learning where you have a fixed dataset). This dependency can lead to a vicious circle: if the agent collects poor quality data (e.g. trajectories with no rewards), it will not improve and will continue to collect bad trajectories.
L27: This factor, among others, explains that results in RL may vary from one run to another (i.e., when only the seed of the pseudo-random generator changes). For this reason, you should always do several runs to obtain quantitative results.
L28: 
L29: Good results in RL generally depend on finding appropriate hyperparameters. Recent algorithms (PPO, SAC, TD3, DroQ) normally require little hyperparameter tuning, however, don’t expect the default ones to work in every environment.
L30: Therefore, we highly recommend that you take a look at the cite12†RL zoo†github.com (or the original papers) for tuned hyperparameters. A best practice when you apply RL to a new problem is to do automatic cite13†hyperparameter optimization†araffin.github.io . Again, this is included in the cite12†RL zoo†github.com .
L31: When applying RL to a custom problem, you should always normalize the input to the agent (e.g. using `VecNormalize` for PPO/A2C) and look at common preprocessing done on other environments (e.g. for cite14†Atari†danieltakeshi.github.io , frame-stack, …). Please refer to Tips and Tricks when creating a custom environment paragraph below for more advice related to custom environments.
L32: ### Current Limitations of RLcite15† L33: 
L34: You have to be aware of the current cite16†limitations†www.alexirpan.com of reinforcement learning.
L35: Model-free RL algorithms (i.e. all the algorithms implemented in SB3) are usually sample inefficient. They require a lot of samples (sometimes millions of interactions) to learn anything useful. That’s why most of the successes in RL were achieved on games or in simulation only. For instance, in this cite17†work†www.youtube.com by ETH Zurich, the ANYmal robot was trained in simulation only, and then tested in the real world.
L36: As general advice, to obtain better performance, you should increase the budget of the agent (number of training timesteps).
L37: In order to achieve the desired behavior, expert knowledge is often required to design an adequate reward function. This reward engineering (or RewArt as coined by cite18†Freek Stulp†www.freekstulp.net ), necessitates several iterations. As a good example of reward shaping, you can take a look at cite19†Deep Mimic paper†xbpeng.github.io which combines imitation learning and reinforcement learning to do acrobatic moves.
L38: A final limitation of RL is the instability of training. That is, you can observe a huge drop in performance during training. This behavior is particularly present in `DDPG`, that’s why its extension `TD3` tries to tackle that issue. Other methods, such as `TRPO` or `PPO` use a trust region to minimize this problem by avoiding too large updates.
L39: ### How to evaluate an RL algorithm?cite20† L40: 
L41: Note
L42: 
L43: Pay attention to environment wrappers when evaluating your agent and comparing results to others’ results. Modifications to episode rewards or lengths may also affect evaluation results which may not be desirable. Check `evaluate_policy` helper function in cite21†Evaluation Helper section.
L44: Because most algorithms use exploration noise during training, you need a separate test environment to evaluate the performance of your agent at a given time. It is recommended to periodically evaluate your agent for `n` test episodes (`n` is usually between 5 and 20) and average the reward per episode to have a good estimate.
L45: 
L46: Note
L47: 
L48: We provide an `EvalCallback` for doing such evaluation. You can read more about it in the cite22†Callbacks section.
L49: As some policies are stochastic by default (e.g. A2C or PPO), you should also try to set `deterministic=True` when calling the `.predict()` method, this frequently leads to better performance. Looking at the training curve (episode reward as a function of timesteps) is a good proxy but underestimates the agent’s true performance.
L50: 
L51: We highly recommend reading cite23†Empirical Design in Reinforcement Learning†arxiv.org , as it provides valuable insights for best practices when running RL experiments.
L52: We also suggest reading cite24†Deep Reinforcement Learning that Matters†arxiv.org for a good discussion about RL evaluation, and cite25†Rliable: Better Evaluation for Reinforcement Learning†araffin.github.io for comparing results.
L53: 
L54: You can also take a look at this cite26†blog post†openlab-flowers.inria.fr and this cite27†issue†github.com by Cédric Colas.
L55: ## Which algorithm should I use?cite28† L56: 
L57: There is no silver bullet in RL, you can choose one or the other depending on your needs and problems. The first distinction comes from your action space, i.e., do you have discrete (e.g. LEFT, RIGHT, …) or continuous actions (ex: go to a certain speed)?
L58: 
L59: Some algorithms are only tailored for one or the other domain: `DQN` supports only discrete actions, while `SAC` is restricted to continuous actions.
L60: The second difference that will help you decide is whether you can parallelize your training or not. If what matters is the wall clock training time, then you should lean towards `A2C` and its derivatives (PPO, …). Take a look at the cite29†Vectorized Environments to learn more about training with multiple workers.
L61: To accelerate training, you can also take a look at cite30†SBX†github.com , which is SB3 + Jax, it has less features than SB3 but can be up to 20x faster than SB3 PyTorch thanks to JIT compilation of the gradient update.
L62: 
L63: In sparse reward settings, we recommend using dedicated methods like HER (see below) or population-based algorithms like ARS (available in our cite31†contrib repo .
L64: 
L65: To sum up:
L66: ### Discrete Actionscite32† L67: 
L68: Note
L69: 
L70: This covers `Discrete`, `MultiDiscrete`, `Binary` and `MultiBinary` spaces
L71: 
L72: #### Discrete Actions - Single Processcite33† L73: 
L74: `DQN` with extensions (double DQN, prioritized replay, …) are the recommended algorithms. We notably provide `QR-DQN` in our cite31†contrib repo . `DQN` is usually slower to train (regarding wall clock time) but is the most sample efficient (because of its replay buffer).
L75: #### Discrete Actions - Multiprocessedcite34† L76: 
L77: You should give a try to `PPO` or `A2C`.
L78: 
L79: ### Continuous Actionscite35† L80: #### Continuous Actions - Single Processcite36† L81: 
L82: Note
L83: 
L84: We have a video on cite6†Recent Advances in RL for Continuous Control (SOTA)†www.youtube.com with cite7†slides (early 2026 update)†araffin.github.io .
L85: 
L86: Current State Of The Art (SOTA) algorithms are `SAC`, `TD3`, `CrossQ` and `TQC` (available in our cite31†contrib repo and cite37†SBX (SB3 + Jax) repo ). Please use the hyperparameters in the cite12†RL zoo†github.com for best results.
L87: If you want an extremely sample-efficient algorithm, we recommend using the cite38†DroQ configuration†twitter.com in cite30†SBX†github.com (it does many gradient steps per step in the environment).

L90: Take a look at `PPO`, `TRPO` (available in our cite31†contrib repo ) or `A2C`. Again, don’t forget to take the hyperparameters from the cite12†RL zoo†github.com for continuous actions problems (cf Bullet envs).

L101: The `batch_size` is an important hyperparameter for experiments with cite41†HER L102: ## Tips and Tricks when creating a custom environmentcite42† L103: 
L104: If you want to learn about how to create a custom environment, we recommend you read this cite43†page . We also provide a cite44†colab notebook†colab.research.google.com for a concrete example of creating a custom gym environment.
L105: 
L106: Some basic advice:
L107: 
L108:   * always normalize your observation space if you can, i.e. if you know the boundaries
L109:   * normalize your action space and make it symmetric if it is continuous (see potential problem below) A good practice is to rescale your actions so that they lie in [-1, 1]. This does not limit you, as you can easily rescale the action within the environment.
L110: 
L111:   * start with a shaped reward (i.e. informative reward) and a simplified version of your problem
L112: 
L113:   * debug with random actions to check if your environment works and follows the gym interface (with `check_env`, see below)
L114: Two important things to keep in mind when creating a custom environment are avoiding breaking the Markov assumption and properly handle termination due to a timeout (maximum number of steps in an episode). For example, if there is a time delay between action and observation (e.g. due to wifi communication), you should provide a history of observations as input.
L115: Termination due to timeout (max number of steps per episode) needs to be handled separately. You should return `truncated = True`. If you are using the gym `TimeLimit` wrapper, this will be done automatically. You can read cite45†Time Limit in RL†arxiv.org , take a look at the cite4†Designing and Running Real-World RL Experiments video†youtu.be or cite2†RL Tips and Tricks video†www.youtube.com for more details.

L120:     env = CustomEnv(arg1, ...)

L135: 
L136: Why should I normalize the action space?
L137: Most reinforcement learning algorithms rely on a cite46†Gaussian distribution†araffin.github.io (initially centered at 0 with std 1) for continuous actions. So, if you forget to normalize the action space when using a custom environment, this can cite46†harm learning†araffin.github.io and can be difficult to debug (cf attached image and cite47†issue #473†github.com ).
L138: 
L139: cite48†Image: ../_images/mistake.png L140: Another consequence of using a Gaussian distribution is that the action range is not bounded. That’s why clipping is usually used as a bandage to stay in a valid interval. A better solution would be to use a squashing function (cf `SAC`) or a Beta distribution (cf cite49†issue #112†github.com ).
L141: 
L142: Note
L143: 
L144: This statement is not true for `DDPG` or `TD3` because they don’t rely on any probability distribution.
L145: ## Tips and Tricks when implementing an RL algorithmcite50† L146: 
L147: Note
L148: 
L149: We have a cite51†video on YouTube about reliable RL†www.youtube.com that covers this section in more details. You can also find the cite52†slides online†araffin.github.io .
L150: 
L151: When you try to reproduce a RL paper by implementing the algorithm, the cite53†nuts and bolts of RL research†joschu.net by John Schulman are quite useful (cite54†video†www.youtube.com ).
L152: 
L153: We recommend following those steps to have a working RL algorithm:
L154:   1. Read the original paper several times
L155: 
L156:   2. Read existing implementations (if available)
L157: 
L158:   3. Try to have some “signs of life” on toy problems
L159: 
L160:   4. Validate the implementation by making it run on harder and harder envs (you can compare results against the RL zoo). You usually need to run hyperparameter optimization for that step.
L161: You need to be particularly careful on the shape of the different objects you are manipulating (a broadcast mistake will fail silently cf. cite55†issue #75†github.com ) and when to stop the gradient propagation.
L162: 
L163: Don’t forget to handle termination due to timeout separately (see remark in the custom environment section above), you can also take a look at cite56†Issue #284†github.com and cite57†Issue #633†github.com .
